# Story structures

Pick **one** primary structure per piece. A short structure (such as ABT) may be nested inside a section of a longer one, but the piece has one spine.

## Selection table

Pick in this order:

1. **Genre.** If the piece matches a genre row, the genre decides, whatever its purpose.
2. **Purpose.** Only if no genre row matches, choose by what the piece must do to the audience.
3. **Length modifier.** Always apply it, after step 1 or 2.

Use the escape hatch only when the structure's "avoid when" note applies.

| Genre | Default | Escape hatch |
|---|---|---|
| Customer case study, testimonial, marketing narrative | **Customer-as-hero** | Story Spine if the customer isn't the protagonist |
| Explainer or onboarding story whose main job is to teach a concept, even when told as fiction | **Mystery** | Kishōtenketsu when there's no natural conflict |
| Fiction, memoir, personal essay, lore | **Story Circle** | Story Spine if the protagonist doesn't change; Three-act for long plots; Hero's Journey only for long-form transformation epics |
| Speech or toast | **Story Spine**, compressed, with a callback ending | — |
| Incident, project, retro, anecdote, origin story: something that happened, even when it teaches or persuades | **Story Spine** | Three-act for pieces over ~2000 words |

| Purpose (talks, keynotes, posts, and anything else) | Default | Escape hatch |
|---|---|---|
| Recommend a decision to executives or a busy reader | **SCQA** | — |
| Persuade a group to change (vision, strategy, proposal) | **Sparkline** | — |
| Teach how something works (explainer, onboarding, concept) | **Mystery** | Kishōtenketsu when there's no natural conflict |

**Length modifier.** Under ~2 minutes or ~300 words (an update, an intro, a pitch opener, an abstract), compress the chosen structure to its ABT form: And (the setup), But (the problem), Therefore (the change). For a toast or flash fiction, keep the genre structure but use one scene only.

## ABT: And, But, Therefore

*Randy Olson, "Houston, We Have a Narrative".* Beats: **And** sets up the situation (facts that agree). **But** is the problem or contradiction. **Therefore** is the consequence or action.

- **Use when:** you have seconds, or as the spine of a paragraph inside a larger piece.
- **Avoid when:** the audience needs to *experience* the change, not just hear it.
- **Example:** "Our deploys took 40 minutes, *and* every team waited on them. *But* half that time was a test suite no one had looked at since 2019. *Therefore* we split it, and deploys now take 11."

## SCQA: Situation, Complication, Question, Answer

*Barbara Minto, "The Pyramid Principle".* Beats: **Situation** (agreed context). **Complication** (what changed or is threatened). **Question** (the one the complication raises). **Answer** (your recommendation), followed by supporting points.

- **Use when:** the reader wants the conclusion fast and the story only needs to earn it.
- **Avoid when:** you need emotional engagement; SCQA informs more than it moves.

## Sparkline: what is versus what could be

*Nancy Duarte, "Resonate".* Alternate between **what is** (the current reality, its pain) and **what could be** (the better future), several times, building contrast. End with a **call to action** and a vision of the **new bliss**.

- **Use when:** asking an audience to change behavior or support a change.
- **Avoid when:** there's nothing to ask of the audience.
- **Craft note:** each "what is" should be concrete (a real moment, a number); each "what could be" should be vivid enough to want.

## Customer-as-hero

*Adapted from Donald Miller's StoryBrand.* Beats: a **customer** with a **want**, faces a **problem** (external, and how it made them feel), meets a **guide** (you, showing empathy and authority), who gives them a **plan**, and calls them to **act**. Show both the **failure** they avoided and the **success** they reached.

- **Use when:** you are the vendor, team, or tool and they are the beneficiary.
- **Avoid when:** you are the protagonist (then use Story Spine). Putting yourself as hero in a customer story is the most common failure here.

## Mystery: the explainer structure

Beats: **the question** (a puzzle the audience cares about: "how does your laptop find google.com in 20 milliseconds?"). **The obvious answer, and why it fails.** **Clues**: the pieces of the mechanism, each revealed when the previous one raises a question. **The reveal**: how it actually works, now obvious in hindsight. **So what**: why knowing this changes something for the audience.

- **Use when:** teaching. Curiosity is the tension, and each clue is a small payoff.
- **Avoid when:** the audience didn't come to learn; if they need to act, use Sparkline.
- **Craft note:** a protagonist (a packet, a new hire, a request) gives the mechanism a body to travel through.
- **Accuracy:** the puzzle must be true as stated. "Heavier things fall faster" is a belief to break, not a premise to state as fact. Frame wrong beliefs as what someone *thought*.

## Kishōtenketsu

*Four-part structure from Chinese and Japanese poetry.* Beats: **Ki** (introduction). **Shō** (development). **Ten** (a twist: an unexpected, seemingly unrelated element). **Ketsu** (reconciliation: the twist and the setup combine into a new understanding).

- **Use when:** there's no natural conflict and you don't want to invent one; good for reflective or surprising explainers.
- **Avoid when:** the material has a real conflict. Use it.

## Story Spine

*Kenn Adams, improv; popularized by Pixar.* "Once upon a time… Every day… **Until one day**… **Because of that**… **Because of that**… **Until finally**… And ever since then…"

- **Use when:** recounting events. It forces causality ("because of that") and a before and after.
- **Avoid when:** the piece is mainly an argument (SCQA) or an explanation (Mystery).
- **Example (origin story):** Every day, our founder spent her evenings rebuilding the same spreadsheet for her bakery's flour orders. Until one day, a supplier doubled the price mid-month and the sheet couldn't cope. Because of that, she wrote a tiny script to compare suppliers. Because of that, three other bakers asked to use it. Until finally, she quit the bakery to build it full time. Ever since then, every feature starts with a baker's actual spreadsheet.

## Three-act

Beats: **Setup** (the world and the protagonist's want; ends with an inciting incident). **Confrontation** (rising obstacles, a midpoint shift, the low point). **Resolution** (the climax decision; the new normal).

- **Use when:** the piece is long enough to need a midpoint and a low point.
- **Avoid when:** under ~1000 words. It becomes a sketch of acts with no room inside them.

## Story Circle

*Dan Harmon, simplified from Campbell.* Eight beats: (1) a character in a zone of comfort, (2) wants something, (3) enters an unfamiliar situation, (4) adapts to it, (5) gets what they wanted, (6) pays a heavy price for it, (7) returns to the familiar, (8) changed.

- **Use when:** character change is the point (fiction, memoir, personal essays).
- **Avoid when:** the protagonist doesn't change. Use Story Spine.

## Hero's Journey

*Joseph Campbell; Christopher Vogler's 12-stage version.* Ordinary world, call to adventure, refusal of the call, meeting the mentor, crossing the threshold, tests, allies and enemies, approach to the inmost cave, the ordeal, reward, the road back, resurrection, return with the elixir.

- **Use when:** long-form transformation stories (a novel, a saga, a campaign's lore).
- **Avoid when:** almost everything else. Forced onto a project retro or a case study, it reads as parody. Story Circle covers the same arc in less space.
