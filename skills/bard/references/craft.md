# Craft: making it captivating and making it teach

Technique for drafting (step 5) and revising (step 6). Each section names the failure it prevents.

## Openings

*Prevents: the audience deciding in the first ten seconds that this is background.*

Open with one of these, drawn from the ledger:

- **In the middle of the action**: the worst moment, then step back. "At 2:14 a.m. the dashboard went green, and that's when we knew something was very wrong."
- **A question the audience can't help answering**: "How many of you have shipped a fix you couldn't explain?"
- **A surprising, specific fact**: "Our most reliable service had never once been tested."
- **The stakes**: what was about to be lost.

Then deliver the context the opening made them want. Cut any sentence before the hook that the audience doesn't need yet.

## Tension

*Prevents: a sequence of events that is accurate and boring.*

- **Want plus obstacle.** Someone wants something specific; something stands in the way. No want, no story.
- **"But" and "therefore", not "and then".** *(Trey Parker and Matt Stone.)* Test each transition between beats. If "and then" fits, the beats aren't causally linked. Find the link or cut a beat.
- **Raise the stakes.** Make clear what happens if they fail, in terms this audience cares about.
- **Open loops.** Raise a question early and answer it late. Close every loop you open.
- **Let it get worse before it gets better.** The low point is what makes the resolution feel earned. Don't skip it to be diplomatic; in blameless contexts, make the system or the assumption the antagonist.

## Specificity

*Prevents: generic prose that could be about anything.*

- Pull details from the ledger, not from imagination: the exact words on the screen, the time, the number.
- Pick the **telling detail**: the one specific thing that implies the rest. Don't list every detail you have.

## Scenes and summary

*Prevents: the story being told about instead of shown.*

A **scene** happens at a specific time and place, with people doing and saying things. **Summary** compresses time. Use scenes for the inciting moment, the turning point, and the low point; use summary for everything between. A piece that is all summary is a report. A piece that is all scene is exhausting and long.

## Character

*Prevents: an audience with no one to care about.*

- Give the protagonist a want the audience recognizes, and at least one flaw or wrong belief. Perfect people don't learn, so the audience doesn't either.
- In explainers, a protagonist can be a person (a new hire), an object (a request, a packet), or the audience itself ("you").
- In persuasive pieces, the audience is the hero (see Customer-as-hero in `references/structures.md`).

## Teaching inside the story

*Prevents: a story that entertains and teaches nothing, or a lecture dressed as a story.*

- **One core idea.** Decide the single thing they must learn. Everything else is support or cut.
- **Make it stick: SUCCESs.** *(Chip and Dan Heath, "Made to Stick".)* Simple (the core), Unexpected (break their prediction to get attention), Concrete (things you can see), Credible (a detail or source they can verify), Emotional (a person they care about), Story. Check the core idea against each.
- **Beat the curse of knowledge.** You know too much to remember what not knowing felt like. Explain each term at the moment it is needed, in words the audience already has. When unsure, test with the audience's likely first question.
- **Break a wrong model first.** Let the character (and audience) hold the common wrong belief, then show it failing. Learning sticks better when it replaces something.
- **Let them predict.** Pause before the reveal ("what would you have done?"). A prediction, even a wrong one, makes the answer memorable.
- **Use a bridge analogy** from the audience's world for the hardest concept, then show where the analogy breaks.
- **The lesson lives at the turn.** The character learns it under pressure; the audience learns it with them. Then state it once, plainly, near the end.

## Endings

*Prevents: the story trailing off, or ending in a lecture.*

- Resolve the want: did they get it, and what did it cost?
- Show the changed state ("ever since then…").
- Call back to the opening image or line. The callback signals that the story is over and rewards attention.
- State the takeaway once (spoken pieces also say it at the turn; see `references/formats.md`). For persuasive pieces, end with the specific action you want.
- Don't end with "In conclusion", a summary of every point, or a list of lessons learned.

## Voice

*Prevents: prose that sounds like a press release.*

Match the occasion: a toast is warm, an incident story is plain and exact, a pitch is confident, an explainer is curious. Pick the tone in the brief and hold it.

## Revision moves

When a check in step 6 fails, the usual fix:

| Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|
| Slow start | Delete the first paragraph; see if the second is the real opening |
| Flat middle | Find the low point in the ledger and dramatize it as a scene |
| "So what?" ending | Return to the takeaway in the brief; end on the changed state plus that one line |
| Too long | Cut whole beats or examples, not words from every sentence |
| Too abstract | Replace each abstract claim with a ledger detail that shows it |
| Lecture-y | Tie each explanation to a moment where the character needs it |
